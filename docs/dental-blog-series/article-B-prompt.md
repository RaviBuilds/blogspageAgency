## The reader and the one thing they should take away

A Hyderabad dentist with a website, an Instagram page and a Google profile, who has heard about the advisory but is not sure what it means for them. They are worried, not hostile. **Takeaway:** a clear, calm way to think about it (**information versus promotion**), a **claims register** they can build in an afternoon, and a **10-point audit** for their website, Google profile and Instagram.

## Angle

This is the only news-led article in the series, so **accuracy beats cleverness**. Open with the date and the facts. Be the calm, careful voice among alarmist explainers. Never scare; never claim certainty the sources do not give. Position Blogspage as a team that builds clinic websites **around** these constraints, but only at the end and lightly.

## Structure (H2s; adapt wording, keep the order)

1. **What the advisory actually says.** Issuer (Ethics and Dental Registration Board of the National Dental Commission), date (14 October 2026), who it applies to, the clauses cited (Chapter VI, Clauses 8.1 to 8.3 of the Revised Dentists (Code of Ethics) Regulations, 2014), the six practices named (see strategy R2), and the consequences stated (show-cause notice, suspension, removal from State and National Register). Short "at a glance" bullets. Link the primary NDC notice if you can verify it; otherwise link Medical Dialogues and say it is trade reporting.
   - **First 120 words** must say plainly: what happened, who it affects, and what the article will give them.
2. **Who regulates what now.** The NDC replaced the DCI on 19 March 2026; as far as you can find, no new code has been notified, so the 2014 Code is the reference (R4, R5). State dental council registration still matters. **The NMC's October 2026 guidelines are for medical practitioners and hospitals, not stand-alone dental clinics** (R6). One paragraph on why dentists should read their own Code rather than borrow doctors' rules.
3. **Why it catches clinics off guard.** Use the Andhra Pradesh study (R7) **once**, labelled clearly as an Andhra Pradesh study and noting you have no Telangana figure. Keep it to two or three sentences. No further statistics anywhere.
4. **A simple test for your website: information or promotion?** Two bulleted lists.
   - *Information a patient can use:* clinic name, address, hours and contact; doctors' names with recognised qualifications and state registration number; plain descriptions of treatments; what to expect at a first visit; factual facilities; how to reach the clinic.
   - *Promotion the advisory cautions against:* superlatives and self-aggrandising claims; misleading or outcome-implying treatment claims; patient testimonials; before-and-after photographs; recorded or broadcast consultations and procedures; inaccurate health information; oversized signage; language that reads as patient solicitation.
   - *Grey areas (say they are unresolved):* Google reviews shown on a clinic website; doctor-explains educational reels; team and clinic photography; listing fees (some explainers read the 2014 Code as allowing factual fee and treatment listings; treat as unconfirmed). Advise confirming with the state dental council.
5. **Instagram, reels and WhatsApp.** What the advisory names about recording and broadcasting. State that, as reported, it does not say patient consent makes recording acceptable, so treat it as higher risk until clarified. Educational content: general, doctor-reviewed, no identifiable patients, no outcome claims. WhatsApp: patient-initiated enquiries are different from broadcasts to people who have not contacted the clinic, which can read as solicitation (mark as our reading).
6. **Patient data: forms, WhatsApp and analytics.** Plain-language DPDP section using R9, including the uncertainty about the timeline. Practical points: collect only what you need, say why, keep symptoms and enquiry text out of analytics, messages sent only by the patient, know who on staff can see enquiries. Do not mention HIPAA.
7. **Build a claims register in one afternoon.** This is the signature, original section. Describe a simple table (as a bulleted or numbered structure, since tables are not supported): **Claim, where it appears, evidence, who approved it, review date.** Give three worked examples:
   - "Registered dentist, BDS": evidence is the state council registration and degree certificate; approved by the dentist; reviewed on a date.
   - "Digital X-ray available": evidence is the equipment actually in the clinic.
   - "Painless root canals": no evidence can support the word, so it is removed or rewritten as a description of what the dentist does to manage comfort.
   Tie it to the doctor-reviewed workflow: **doctor interview, draft, doctor review, publish**, with a named reviewer and a review date on every clinical page.
8. **A 10-point audit for your website, Google profile and Instagram.** A numbered list the reader can do tonight: superlatives; testimonials or before-and-after on any channel; recorded procedure clips; signage; registration number and recognised degrees shown correctly; fee or offer language; health information accuracy and doctor review; forms, WhatsApp and analytics handling of patient data; Google Business Profile description and categories; who owns updates.
9. **What we don't know yet.** Honest list of the unresolved questions (reviews on websites, educational reels, whether a new Code is coming, how councils will enforce). Say the article is dated 1 November 2026, will be updated, and invite corrections.
10. **Closing paragraph, disclosure, and CTA.** Include the one-sentence commercial-interest disclosure. Include the "not legal advice, confirm with your state dental council or a healthcare lawyer" note (it also appears earlier in section 1 or 4).

## Must avoid

- Telling anyone their website or content is illegal or compliant.
- Naming or shaming any real clinic, doctor or competitor.
- Any claim about enforcement in Telangana specifically.
- Recommending testimonials, patient stories, before-and-after, offers or urgency.
- Treating Blogspage's blueprint as the answer to compliance. It is one way of organising information.
